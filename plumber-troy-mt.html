What Makes Troy's Plumbing Different

Troy's housing stock splits into two distinct groups, and each one fails in its own way. Down near the river and along the older residential streets close to downtown, a lot of the homes date back to the town's mining and timber era, which means the same galvanized supply lines and aging cast iron drains I see all over Libby — pipe that's been in the ground or in the walls for 50-plus years and is finally reaching the end of its service life. Up toward Bull Lake, the story is different: seasonal cabins, private wells, and septic systems that sit unused for stretches of the year and then get asked to perform flawlessly the first cold weekend someone shows up to use them.

The river itself adds a wrinkle too. Homes close to the Kootenai River tend to sit on higher water tables, which means sump pumps and floor drains work harder here than they would on higher ground, especially during spring runoff when snowmelt off the Cabinet Mountains pushes groundwater levels up. If your sump pump hasn't been tested in a while, or your basement gets a faint musty smell every spring, that's usually the first sign it's falling behind.

Serving Cabins and Second Homes Near Bull Lake

A fair number of the properties I service around Troy aren't full-time residences — they're cabins and vacation homes clustered around Bull Lake that get heavy use in summer and sit closed up for the rest of the year. That pattern is exactly what causes the most expensive plumbing failures: a water system that wasn't drained and winterized correctly before the owners left, followed by a hard freeze that splits a pipe nobody discovers until the next visit. I handle both sides of that problem — winterizing a system properly before you close up for the season, and doing the spring reopening and repair work when something's already failed.
