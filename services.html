Plumbing and Heating Work Built for Libby's Older Homes and Cold Winters

Most of the plumbing and heating calls I run in Libby, MT come down to two things: age and cold. A lot of housing stock in Lincoln County dates back to the mining and timber boom years, which means galvanized supply lines that are finally rusting through, cast iron drain lines nearing the end of their service life, and water heaters that were installed decades ago and are still limping along. On top of that, Libby sees hard winters with sustained sub-freezing stretches, which puts real stress on exposed pipe, unheated crawlspaces, and older furnaces that were never designed to run this hard for this long.

That combination is exactly what I specialize in. As a Montana-certified Master Plumber with more than 30 years in the trade, I've replaced enough galvanized pipe and diagnosed enough dead furnaces in this county to know where the problems usually hide — and how to fix them without tearing a house apart. Whether you need a same-day repair, a full water heater replacement, or a furnace tune-up before the first hard freeze, I handle it myself, start to finish.

How I Approach a Service Call

Every job starts the same way: a real diagnosis before any parts get replaced. A lot of plumbing and heating problems present with similar symptoms — low water pressure could be a failing pressure regulator, a partially closed valve, or corroded galvanized pipe narrowing from the inside; a furnace short-cycling could be a dirty flame sensor, a failing thermostat, or a restricted air filter. Guessing at the cause and replacing parts until something works costs you more money and more of your time, so I spend the first part of any visit actually figuring out what's wrong before I touch anything.

Once I know what the job is, I explain it in plain language — what's actually failing, why it failed, what the repair involves, and what it's going to cost — before I start any work. There's no dispatch fee that doesn't apply to the job, no upsell to a bigger repair than you actually need, and no leaving a job half-finished because it turned out to be more involved than expected. If a job is going to take longer than expected, you hear about it before it happens, not after.

Repair vs. Replacement

One of the most common questions I get on any service call is whether something's worth repairing or whether it makes more sense to replace it outright. For water heaters, furnaces, and older plumbing fixtures, that decision usually comes down to age, the cost of the specific repair relative to a full replacement, and how much life is realistically left in the rest of the system. I'll walk through that math honestly rather than defaulting to whichever option costs more — sometimes a $150 repair buys you several more years out of a unit, and sometimes it's just delaying an inevitable replacement by a few months. You make the final call, but you'll have real numbers to work from.
